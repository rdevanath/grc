    Disaster Recovery sites

    Key points

    Redudancy vs Disaster Recovery

    • Redundancy duplicates critical elements like servers, power supplies, or network paths to eliminate single points of failure and ensure continuous operation during minor faults. Disaster recovery involves strategies, backups, and procedures to restore data and services following catastrophic events such as cyberattacks or natural disasters.

    • Redundancy aims for high availability with minimal or no interruption via active failover, often within the same site. Disaster recovery targets full restoration after widespread outages, potentially using offsite replicas, and accepts longer recovery times.

    • Load balancing distributes incoming traffic evenly across redundant servers or instances, ensuring no single point overloads and maintaining uptime during component failures. This supports redundancy by leveraging duplicated resources, such as multiple servers in a cluster, for seamless operation. Load balancers route traffic to secondary sites or regions post-disaster, using health checks to detect and failover from failed zones automatically. Global load balancers extend this across regions, minimizing RTO by switching to available backups quickly.

    Hot Site vs Cold Site

    A hot site is a fully equipped backup facility that can take over operations almost immediately after a disaster, while a cold site is just a basic space with minimal infrastructure, requiring significant time and setup before it becomes operational.

    A cold site it has technology at a minimal level. People (trained staff) and processes (detailed recovery procedures) must be brought in during activation, as the site arrives empty and requires significant setup time.

    Feature Hot Site Cold Site
    Definition A continuously running backup site ready to take over operations instantly. A bare facility with basic infrastructure but no active systems.
    Setup Time Minimal — operations can resume within hours or less. Long — may take days or weeks to become functional.
    Infrastructure Fully equipped with hardware, software, networking, and internet connectivity. Only physical space, power, and cooling; equipment must be brought in.
    Data Availability Data is continuously replicated or backed up to the site. No real-time data; requires manual restoration from backups.
    Cost High (due to constant maintenance and readiness). Low (minimal ongoing costs).
    Use Case Mission-critical businesses needing near-zero downtime (banks, hospitals, e-commerce). Organizations with less critical operations that can tolerate downtime.

    Cold Site Limitations

    • Provides only the physical environment (building, power, cooling).
    • Requires transporting servers, installing software, and restoring data before use.
    • Suitable for organizations with lower disaster recovery budgets or less critical workloads.

    Trade-Offs

    • Cost vs. Speed is the main difference:
    • Hot sites are expensive but fast.
    • Cold sites are cheap but slow.
    • Many companies adopt a hybrid approach (warm site) to balance cost and recovery time.

    Risks & Considerations

    • Hot Site: High cost may be unjustifiable for small businesses unless downtime is catastrophic.
    • Cold Site: Risk of prolonged downtime and potential data loss if backups are not current.
    • Decision Point: Evaluate Recovery Time Objective (RTO) and Recovery Point Objective (RPO).
    • Hot sites excel at tight RTO/RPO.
    • Cold sites do not.

    Hot Site vs Mirror Site

    A hot site is a disaster recovery facility that is fully operational and ready to take over business functions immediately after a failure. A mirror site, on the other hand, is an exact replica of a website or system, often used to distribute traffic, reduce latency, or provide redundancy.

    Feature Hot Site Mirror Site
    Purpose Disaster recovery and business continuity Load balancing, redundancy, and faster access
    Scope Entire IT infrastructure (servers, apps, data, networking) Primarily websites or data repositories
    Activation Used only during disasters or outages Active all the time, serving users simultaneously
    Data Sync Data replicated regularly to ensure minimal downtime Data mirrored in real-time or near real-time
    Cost High (requires full infrastructure and maintenance) Moderate (depends on hosting and bandwidth)
    Use Case Banks, hospitals, airlines needing instant failover Large websites, software repositories, or global services like Wikipedia/CDNs

    Risks & Considerations

    • Hot Site: Very expensive to maintain; only justified for mission-critical operations.
    • Mirror Site: Needs constant synchronization; if not properly managed, users may encounter outdated or inconsistent data. Performance overhead may occur if replication isn’t optimized.
    • Alternative Approaches: Some organizations use active-active clusters or geo-redundant architectures instead of a simple mirror site, achieving the same goals with more flexibility.
